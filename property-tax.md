Every Georgia property owner is billed by several governments at once, each setting
its own rate, at different times of year, with separate chances to object. This page
explains the pieces, and the dates on which you can actually do something.

To see what your own county, city and school district charge, and how those rates
have moved since 2020, open your place on the
[Georgia Local Government directory]({{ '/local/' | relative_url }}).

{: .box-note}
**Your assessment notice is not your bill, and it cannot be.** The notice tells you
what your property is worth. Your bill is that value times a millage rate — and the
millage rate is set *months later*, after the window to appeal your value has already
closed. They are two separate decisions, with two separate deadlines, and the second
one is the one almost nobody shows up for.

## What a mill is

A **mill** is one dollar of tax per **$1,000 of assessed value**. A rate of 15 mills
means $15 per $1,000.

Assessed value is not market value. In Georgia, most property is assessed at **40% of
its fair market value** — the Department of Revenue's
[Property Tax Valuation](https://dor.georgia.gov/property-tax-valuation) page states
the 40% rule and cites O.C.G.A. § 48-5-7, which also sets different ratios for a few
specific categories such as rehabilitated historic, brownfield and forest-land
conservation property.

So the arithmetic for most homes is:

> **fair market value × 40% ÷ 1,000 × millage rate = tax**

A house with a $300,000 fair market value has a $120,000 assessed value. At 15 mills
that is **$1,800**. One mill costs that owner $120 a year — or, as a rule of thumb,
**a mill costs about 40¢ per $1,000 of market value.**

Exemptions come off the assessed value *before* the rate is applied, so a homestead
exemption lowers the number the millage rate multiplies. Two houses with identical
market values and identical millage rates can owe very different amounts.

## Who levies on you, and why the rates differ

You are usually paying at least three governments:

- **the county**, at a different rate depending on whether your property is inside a
  city — the "incorporated" and "unincorporated" rates are genuinely different numbers
- **the school district**, which is a separately elected body with its own rate, and is
  often the largest single line
- **your city**, if you are inside one

On top of that a parcel may sit inside a fire district, a hospital or library
district, a special service district, or a tax allocation or community improvement
district. Whether one applies depends on the parcel, not the city — which is why no
single "combined rate" is true for everyone in a county.

Georgia's **state** property tax rate has been zero for years, so the state line on
these reports adds nothing.

## The calendar, and the two deadlines

1. **Your county mails an annual assessment notice.** It states the value the Board of
   Tax Assessors has put on your property.
2. **You have 45 days to appeal that value.** The clock runs from **the date the notice
   was mailed**, not the date you opened it.
3. **The appeal window closes.**
4. **Then the levying authorities set their millage rates** — county commission, school
   board, city council — each after its own advertisement and hearings.
5. **You get a bill.**

Because step 4 happens after step 2, an assessment notice structurally cannot tell you
your final bill. In 2026, state legislation also removed estimated taxes, assessed
values and estimated rollback rates from the notice itself, replacing them with an
estimate of tax savings — Gwinnett County's
[Tax Assessor FAQ](https://www.gwinnettcounty.com/government/departments/financial-services/tax-assessor/faqs)
describes that change.

## Appealing your assessment

File a **written appeal with the County Board of Tax Assessors within 45 days** of the
notice's mailing date. The Department of Revenue's
[Filing a Property Tax Appeal](https://dor.georgia.gov/filing-property-tax-appeal)
page sets out three routes, and they are **not** interchangeable — each can decide
different things:

| Route | What it can decide |
|---|---|
| **County Board of Equalization** | value, uniformity, **taxability**, or **denial of an exemption** |
| **Hearing Officer** | value or uniformity of **non-homestead** real property, where the value exceeds **$500,000** |
| **Arbitration** | **value only** |

Only the Board of Equalization can hear an appeal about whether property is taxable at
all, or about an exemption you were denied. If that is your argument, the other two
routes cannot give you what you want.

If you are still dissatisfied, you may appeal to **superior court** by filing written
notice with the Board of Tax Assessors, generally **within 30 days** of the date the
decision was mailed or hand-delivered. Note one practical catch the DOR page states:
before superior court hears the appeal, the taxes must be paid in an amount equal to
the last year in which taxes were determined to be due.

County practice varies on the details — some require a recent certified appraisal for
arbitration, for instance — so read your own notice and your county's instructions.

## The rate decision: rollback, and the hearings nobody attends

This is the part with real leverage, and it is almost entirely unwatched.

When reassessments push the value of existing property up, a government can collect
more money at the *same* millage rate. Georgia law treats that as a tax increase
unless the rate comes down to match.

**O.C.G.A. § 48-5-32.1** requires each levying and recommending authority to compute a
**rollback rate**: last year's millage rate, minus the millage equivalent of the net
assessed value added by **reassessments of existing real property**. Set the rate at
the rollback figure and the government collects roughly what it did last year. Set it
anywhere above, and the law calls that an increase — whatever the headline rate did.

A government proposing a rate **above** the rollback rate must, per § 48-5-32.1 and
the Department of Revenue's
[compliance guide](https://dor.georgia.gov/document/publication/compliance-guide-advertising-digest-history-and-public-hearings-increase/download)
(revised April 2025):

- **hold three public hearings** on the increase, and take public input
- hold **at least one of them between 6:00 PM and 7:00 PM**, so that working people can
  attend
- **advertise a "Notice of Property Tax Increase" at least one week before each
  hearing** — prominently displayed, **not less than 30 square inches**, and
  **not in the legal-notices section** of the newspaper
- **post that notice on its website** at least one week before each hearing
- issue a **press release** at the same time

Those are checkable requirements. An ad buried in the legal notices, one that runs
smaller than 30 square inches, or a hearing schedule with nothing in the early
evening, is not compliance — Murray County had to re-run an advertisement that came in
just under the 30-square-inch minimum.

Separately, **O.C.G.A. § 48-5-32** requires every levying and recommending authority
to publish a **"Current Tax Digest and Five-Year History of Levy"** at least a week
before it sets the rate: the assessed taxable value by class and in total, the proposed
millage rate, and the values and rates for each of the five preceding years — with the
percentage and dollar increase for each year shown. If you want to know whether your
taxes have been climbing, that advertisement is the official answer, and it is
published before the vote, not after.

## Where to watch for it

A millage decision reaches a public agenda before it reaches your mailbox. The
[Georgia Local Government directory]({{ '/local/' | relative_url }}) carries each
covered county's and city's meeting agendas and minutes, and
[Local taxes &amp; referenda]({{ '/local/topics/local-taxes/' | relative_url }}) tracks
where millage rates, SPLOST and other local tax questions are turning up on those
agendas.

Each place page also shows that jurisdiction's actual millage history — county, school
and city lines, 2020 through the most recent published year — taken from the Georgia
Department of Revenue's own digest files. See
[About the Data]({{ '/about-the-data' | relative_url }}) for how that is built and what
it leaves out.
